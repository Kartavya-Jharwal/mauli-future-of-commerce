## Slide 9
CONCEPT · GOOGLE'S MICRO-MOMENTS (2015)
Four micro-moments: every reflexive reach for the phone is one of four needs
I-want-to-know
Researching or exploring — curious, not yet in purchase mode. Preferences are shaped here long before a cart exists.
"is almond milk good in coffee?"
Brand job: useful, snackable answers — not a hard sell.
I-want-to-go
Looking for a local business or considering buying a product at a nearby store — the digital-to-physical bridge.
"pharmacy open now near me"
Brand job: accurate listings, maps presence, local inventory ads.
I-want-to-do
Needs help completing a task or trying something new — "how-to" intent, often mid-task and on mobile.
"how to fix a leaking tap video"
Brand job: how-to content that shows the product in use.
I-want-to-buy
Ready to purchase — needs help deciding what to buy or how to buy it. The moment of highest commercial value.
"iPhone 17 best price"
Brand job: clear price and stock, fast checkout, no surprises.
Source: 
Think with Google (Sridhar Ramaswamy), 2015
 — "intent-rich moments when decisions are made and preferences shaped"

## Slide 11
THEORY · ELABORATION LIKELIHOOD MODEL (ELM)
Two routes to "yes": why some shoppers read specs and others read stars
Richard E. Petty & John T. Cacioppo, 1986 — Communication and Persuasion; Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, Vol. 19.
Persuasive message — ad, product page, review
Motivation × ability to process?
CENTRAL ROUTE — high elaboration (high motivation + ability)
Argument quality decides: facts, spec comparisons, deep reviews, warranty terms. Attitude change is 
durable
 and predicts behaviour.
PERIPHERAL ROUTE — low elaboration
Cues decide: star rating and count, "only 3 left" badges, influencer imagery, one-tap buy. Attitude change is 
temporary and fragile
.
Commerce application:
 one product page must serve both routes — and the same shopper switches routes by category. Segment by 
decision context
, not demographics. Bonus insight: fake-scarcity cues "work" briefly — until central processing catches up and trust erodes.

## Slide 12
THEORY APPLIED · ELM IN THE AISLE
High involvement reads the fine print; low involvement reads the room
Buying a laptop
₹80,000 · three-year commitment · high perceived risk
Route taken: CENTRAL
 — the shopper is motivated and able to process.
Cues that decide:
 spec comparison tables, deep user reviews, expert benchmarks, warranty and return terms.
Page design implication:
 dense factual detail, side-by-side comparison tools, Q&A sections, downloadable spec sheets. Surface the arguments; do not distract from them.
Buying a snack
₹40 · three-second decision · zero perceived risk
Route taken: PERIPHERAL
 — nobody elaborates over a ₹40 impulse.
Cues that decide:
 star rating plus count, "only 3 left", appetising imagery, influencer tie-in, one-tap buy.
Page design implication:
 big imagery, visible social proof, frictionless checkout. Every extra field is a chance to lose an impulse that was never deeply reasoned.
JUDGMENT CRITERIA — WHICH ROUTE WILL YOUR SHOPPER TAKE?
Diagnose involvement by 
decision context
 — price, risk, expertise, time pressure — not by demographics. The same shopper is central-route for a laptop at 11:00 and peripheral-route for a snack at 12:30.

## Slide 13
THEORY · FOGG BEHAVIOR MODEL (B=MAP)
B = M·A·P: behaviour fires only when motivation, ability and a prompt collide
BJ Fogg, Stanford — "A Behavior Model for Persuasive Design", 2009; popularised in Tiny Habits (2019).
MOTIVATION ↑ high
ABILITY → easy to do
the action line
ABOVE THE LINE — behaviour happens
below the line — it doesn't
1-Click reorder
30-field checkout form
cart reminder 1 hour later — the prompt tips it over
M — Motivation.
 The desire to act; scarcity, social proof, and rewards can raise it — briefly.
A — Ability.
 How easy the action is. The highest-leverage lever: 
"make it easy" beats "make it desirable."
P — Prompt.
 The trigger that fires the behaviour: a CTA, a notification, a reminder. Useless below the action line.
All three must converge 
at the same moment
. No motivation rescues a hard action; no prompt fires without some motivation.
Commerce application:
 Amazon's 1-Click ordering (patented 1999) is the canonical ability play — the motivation already existed; the patent simply removed the friction.

## Slide 14
THEORY APPLIED · CHECKOUT DESIGN
Checkout is applied Fogg: raise ability, protect motivation, time the prompt
RAISE ABILITY — the highest-leverage lever
1-Click ordering (Amazon, patented 1999) · Apple Pay / Google Pay / UPI QR · guest checkout · address autofill. Every removed field moves the shopper right on the ability axis.
PROTECT MOTIVATION — don't kill desire at the prompt moment
Show the full price early. Unexpected extra costs are the top abandonment reason (48%) — the total changed exactly when the prompt fired, and motivation collapsed.
TIME THE PROMPT — fire when M and A are present
Cart-abandonment notification within the hour; replenishment prompt when the product is predicted to run out; exit-intent reminder before the tab closes.
~70%
average online cart abandonment rate (mobile ~80% vs desktop ~66%)
48%
abandon because of unexpected extra costs — the #1 reason
Source: 
Baymard Institute cart-abandonment research, 2025
Diagnosis recipe: find where carts die, ask which of M, A, or P failed at that moment — then fix exactly one variable and test.

## Slide 15
CONCEPT · CHOICE OVERLOAD (THE "JAM STUDY")
24 jams drew a crowd; 6 jams sold ten times more
3%
bought after stopping at the 24-jam table
30%
bought after stopping at the 6-jam table
The big display attracted more attention (60% vs 40% stopped) — and converted ten times worse. Attraction ≠ conversion.
Choice overload:
 beyond a point, more options reduce purchase likelihood and post-choice satisfaction.
Boundary:
 meta-analyses find the average effect near zero — expertise, category, and option differentiation moderate it. Manage decision effort; don't chase a magic number of options.
Sources: 
Iyengar & Lepper, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2000 (DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.79.6.995)
; Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd, JCR 2010; Chernev et al. meta-analysis, 2015

## Slide 16
STRATEGY · FIGHTING CHOICE OVERLOAD
Curation beats abundance: four ways storefronts shrink the shelf
CURATE
 — show a credible few, not everything.
 "Top 3 picks", editor's choice, bestseller badges. Amazon's "Best Seller" badge and default sort compress millions of SKUs into a trustworthy shortlist before the shopper ever scrolls.
DEFAULT
 — preselect the sensible option.
 Recommended sort order, pre-chosen variant, pre-filled delivery slot. Defaults convert because "deciding not to choose" is itself a choice most shoppers avoid making.
FILTER
 — let the shopper shrink the shelf themselves.
 Guided-selling quizzes and faceted filters: Sephora's shade-finder turns "which of 200 lipsticks?" into three matches in under a minute.
RECOMMEND
 — let behaviour do the curating.
 Netflix's personalised rows and Amazon's "recommended for you" are curation computed from what shoppers actually did — the bridge to Session B's recommender systems.
Caveat from the evidence:
 overload effects vary by expertise and category — curate, don't amputate. Keep a "see all" escape hatch for expert shoppers who want the 24-jam table.

## Slide 18
TECHNOLOGY · THE MEASUREMENT STACK
The measurement stack: what happened, why, and who to act on
GA4 (Google Analytics 4) — WHAT happened.
 Event-based analytics: every view, scroll, click, and purchase is an event; engagement rate replaces bounce rate; funnel and exploration reports trace paths.
~49% of sites with a known analytics tool · 14M+ sites

Hotjar: 1.3M+ websites · 7.5M+ heatmaps

 Unifies data from every source into persistent, consented, individual-level profiles and pushes them to email, ads, and personalisation engines in real time.

The cues you see (ELM, peripheral route)

"17 people are looking at this hotel"

social proof

The machine you don't see
~1,000 concurrent A/B tests

Booking assumes 
~90% of tests fail

75 countries / 43 languages

## Slide 26
DEFINITIONS · THREE DIFFERENT PROMISES
Customised, personalised, hyper-personalised: three different promises
Customisation
 — the user explicitly configures the product or experience: build-your-own trainers, choose your toppings, arrange your dashboard. 
The customer does the work.
Example: picking your laptop's RAM and storage at checkout.
Personalisation
 — the system tailors content or offers using declared and observed data, via segments and rules: your name, your cart, your browse history. 
The system does the work — from what it already knows.
Example: an abandoned-cart email with your exact item inside.
Hyper-personalisation
 — real-time, individual-level tailoring using behavioural + contextual data and AI: the right product, message, and moment computed for you, 
now
 — beyond "Hi {first_name}".
Example: a homepage re-ranked for this visitor, this device, this hour.
Where the boundary lies:
 customisation asks; personalisation remembers; hyper-personalisation predicts. Each step up needs more data — and more trust.

## Slide 27
FRAMEWORK · PERSONALISATION MATURITY MODEL
The maturity model: each stage funds the next stage's data
increasing data sophistication →
1 · SEGMENT-BASED
Same message to broad groups ("women 25–34, metro").
Data: demographics, campaign tools. Ex: classic email blasts.
2 · 1:1 RULES-BASED
Individual triggers from explicit data — name, cart, browse.
Data: CRM + marketing automation, web analytics. Ex: abandoned-cart email with your exact item.
3 · PREDICTIVE
ML infers what you'll want next from behavioural patterns at scale.
Data: recommender systems, propensity models, CDP. Ex: Netflix rows, Amazon "recommended for you", Stitch Fix.
4 · AGENTIC
 
THE FRONTIER
An AI agent holds goals and acts across steps — researches, compares, negotiates, buys (with permission).
Data: LLM agents, tool use, payment delegation. Ex: Rufus, ChatGPT Instant Checkout, Perplexity Buy with Pro.
The moat is the data flywheel:
 more users → more data → better recommendations → more users. Most mid-market retailers sit at stage 1–2; the leaders climbed by letting each stage fund the next stage's data.

## Slide 32
DEFINITIONS · VOCABULARY OF THE AGENTIC ERA
Two terms to master: AI shopping agent and next-best-action
DEFINITION 1
AI shopping agent
 — an AI system that pursues a shopping goal on a user's behalf across multiple steps: understanding intent, researching options, comparing trade-offs, and (with authorisation) executing purchase and payment — rather than simply answering a single query.
Scenario:
 "Find a carry-on under $150, rated 4★+, delivered by Friday" — the agent compares 40 listings and checks out. 
(Illustrative)
DEFINITION 2
Next-best-action (NBA)
 — a decisioning approach that computes, for each customer at each interaction, the single most valuable action for the business to take — recommend, offer, educate, or 
stay silent
 — using propensity models and business rules.
Scenario:
 for a churn-risk subscriber, the NBA says "educate about a new feature" — not "discount". 
(Illustrative)
Where the boundary lies:
 a chatbot answers; an agent 
acts
. Segmentation treats groups; NBA computes for one customer at one moment — and "do nothing" can be the winning action.

## Slide 35
STRATEGY · MANAGING THE TRADE-OFF
Five rules that keep personalisation on the right side of the line
1 · Value exchange first.
 Ask for data only when the payoff is explicit, immediate, and proportionate. Stitch Fix collects 85+ style data points willingly — because every answer visibly makes the next box better.
2 · First- and zero-party over third-party.
 Quizzes, preference centres, and loyalty programs beat bought data. Regulation (GDPR, CCPA, India's DPDP Act) and platform shifts (post-cookie, ATT) all push toward consented data.
3 · Run the creepiness test.
 Would the customer be surprised that this data exists, or is used this way? If yes — redesign before you ship. Surprise is the leading indicator of backlash.
4 · Make transparency a feature.
 "Recommended because you watched X" — Netflix explains its rows and Amazon labels its placements. Explanation raises trust 
and
 click-through at the same time.
5 · Meet the regulatory baseline — and watch the frontier.
 GDPR-style consent, purpose limitation, right to explanation. Agents add open questions: who is liable when your AI agent buys the wrong thing? Regulation is nascent as of 2026.

## Slide 43
SUMMARY · SESSION B
Session B takeaways: predict precisely, but keep the shopper's trust
1 ·
 The maturity model is a staircase, not a switch.
 Segment → 1:1 rules → predictive → agentic; each stage funds the next stage's data, and the moat is the flywheel, not the algorithm.
2 ·
 Recommenders think two ways.
 Collaborative ("people like you") vs content-based ("similar to what you liked"); hybrid wins, and cold start is a data problem with known mitigations — quizzes, fallbacks, feedback games.
3 ·
 Personalisation is expected and it pays — measured honestly.
 71% expect it; typical lift is 10–15%; but Amazon's "35%" is a 2013 estimate and holdout-tested lift is often 5–15%. Attribution ≠ causation.
4 ·
 Privacy is the binding constraint.
 Value exchange must be explicit, immediate, and proportionate; prefer zero/first-party data; make transparency a feature, not a disclaimer.
5 ·
 Agents are the next interface.
 When AI shops on the shopper's behalf, brands compete to be chosen by machines — AEO is the new SEO.
