## Slide 16

TOPIC 1 · DEFINITION

What exactly counts as e-commerce?

DEFINITION

E-commerce (electronic commerce) is the buying and selling of goods or services, or the transmission of funds or data, over an electronic network — primarily the internet. It spans B2C, B2B, C2C and D2C transactions on any device.

Where the boundary lies — it counts

Buying shoes on a brand's website or app (B2C)

A retailer ordering stock through Alibaba (B2B)

Selling your bike on a marketplace (C2C)

In-app checkout inside TikTok Shop (still e-commerce — newer interface)

Where the boundary lies — it doesn't

Researching online, paying in store (that's web-influenced retail)

Digital ads and marketing (they drive commerce; they aren't the transaction)

Pure in-store card payments (electronic payment, but the sale is physical)

Intuitive test: follow the money, not the screen. If the transaction itself — order and payment — is completed over the network, it is e-commerce, whatever device or app carried it.

## Slide 20

TOPIC 1 · ERA 1 · PRE-1994

Brick-and-mortar built the playbook every later era borrows

What defined the era

Interface: the physical store; catalogue retail as the long-distance variant

Assortment limited by geography — you sell what fits on shelves, to whoever can reach you

Scale game: discount logistics + store networks (Sam Walton's model) made Walmart the #1 global retailer

Its hard limits

Shelf space caps selection; the "long tail" can't be stocked

Price discovery costs the customer time and travel

Opening hours and location gate every purchase

No behavioural data beyond the loyalty card

What still persists — the layer never died

About 80% of global retail is still transacted in person in 2025. Stores have also become digital infrastructure: Walmart's ~4,600 US stores double as fulfilment nodes for its online business (~6.4% of US e-commerce, #2 behind Amazon). The continuum's first lesson appears here already — new channels sit on top of old ones.

## Slide 21

TOPIC 1 · ERA 2 · 1994–2007

E-commerce: the browser removed the shelf-space constraint

Amazon — the catalogue play

Founded July 1994; opened 1995 as "Earth's Biggest Bookstore" — a selection no physical shop could hold. Marketplace (2000), Prime (2005) and AWS (2006) followed. Today it holds ~37.6% of US e-commerce.

Lesson: first-mover advantage compounded by relentless infrastructure investment.

eBay — the marketplace play

Launched as AuctionWeb in September 1995: no inventory at all — the website itself was the market. C2C trading at national scale was suddenly viable, and PayPal (1998) gave it a payment rail.

Lesson: the network is the store; trust mechanisms (ratings, escrow) are the real product.

What changed for the shopper

Selection became effectively unlimited (the "long tail" could finally be sold), and price transparency arrived: comparing ten sellers took minutes, not a day of shop visits. Two business-model archetypes were born that still structure the industry — the catalogue retailer (Amazon) and the marketplace (eBay, later Alibaba's Taobao, 2003).

Sources: company histories per research brief §1.5/§1.8; Statista via SellersCommerce, 2025 (US market share).

## Slide 25

TOPIC 1 · GLOSSARY

Seven terms, precisely defined — learn them once, use them all course

Term

Definition and one example

E-commerce

Buying/selling goods, services, funds or data over an electronic network — primarily the internet; B2C, B2B, C2C, D2C, any device. Example: any Amazon order.

M-commerce

The subset of e-commerce transacted on handheld devices via mobile web or apps; marked by location-awareness, push notifications, biometric payment. Example: ordering in the Starbucks app.

Social commerce

Buying and selling directly within social platforms — discovery, evaluation and checkout in the same app. Example: TikTok Shop. Distinct from social-influenced commerce, where social only sends traffic elsewhere.

Conversational commerce

Commerce through dialogue interfaces — messaging apps, chatbots, voice assistants — where the conversation itself is the storefront. Example: reordering via WhatsApp chat or Alexa.

Agentic commerce

An AI agent autonomously performs multi-step shopping tasks — interpreting goals, searching, comparing and (with permission) transacting — on the buyer's behalf. Example: ChatGPT Instant Checkout.

Headless commerce

Architecture decoupling the customer-facing front end from back-end commerce logic, joined by APIs — one engine can serve web, app, kiosk, voice or AI-agent fronts. Example: a Shopify back end powering a custom app.

Omnichannel / channel mix

Deliberate allocation of assortment, marketing and fulfilment across channels, managed as one integrated customer experience rather than competing silos. Example: buy online, pick up in store.

## Slide 26

TOPIC 1 · THEORY · ROGERS, 1962

Diffusion of Innovation: adoption is a bell curve, growth an S

The theory in one paragraph

Everett M. Rogers, Diffusion of Innovations (1962; 5th ed. 2003), synthesised 500+ diffusion studies. Any population adopts a new technology in a predictable order — innovators (2.5%) → early adopters (13.5%) → early majority (34%) → late majority (34%) → laggards (16%) — so cumulative adoption always traces an S-curve.

Five attributes set the speed

Relative advantage · compatibility · complexity (reversed) · trialability · observability

Chasm (Moore, 1991): many technologies stall between early adopters and the early majority

Source: Rogers, E. M., Diffusion of Innovations, 1962/2003 (Free Press) — adopter shares are the book's canonical figures; Moore, G., Crossing the Chasm, 1991.

## Slide 28

TOPIC 1 · EVIDENCE

The S-curve in the wild: US e-commerce share, 2013–2024

How to read it

Steady climb, then shock: ~0.8 points a year until 2019, then COVID added ~4 points in one year (+41.7% YoY in 2020).

Then reversion: share flatlined 2020–22 — part of the "forever bump" was a pull-forward, not a step change.

Shape check: the slow start, acceleration and inflection match Rogers' S-curve remarkably well.

## Slide 30

TOPIC 1 · STRATEGY

First mover or fast follower? Arrival order doesn't decide — lock-in does

When first movers win

Mechanism: brand becomes the category; data and network effects lock in users and suppliers.

Evidence:

Amazon = "online shopping" for a generation; ~37.6% of US e-commerce three decades on

Alibaba's merchant network made it unassailable in China

UPI set the standard for Indian real-time payments

When fast followers win

Mechanism: the pioneer pays for customer education and infrastructure; the follower copies what works with lower risk.

Evidence:

Google AdWords followed Overture's paid search

Walmart+ followed Prime with 15 years of logistics learning

Flipkart (2007, 12+ years after Amazon) became India's #1 marketplace via cash-on-delivery and vernacular localisation

The judgement criteria — ask two questions

① Are network effects or data lock-in strong? Then being first compounds — move early. ② Was the pioneer's main cost educating the market, and are switching costs low? Then the follower inherits the demand the pioneer paid to create. Counter-examples to remember: eBay lost China to Taobao; MySpace preceded Facebook — being first is not a moat by itself.

## Slide 31

TOPIC 1 · STRATEGY

Channel-mix planning: five steps, in this order — hype last

01

Audit. Measure current revenue and margin by channel — including the marketplace vs D2C split. You cannot sequence what you haven't separated.

02

Match. Map interfaces to customer jobs-to-be-done: replenishment → voice/app subscription; discovery → social; consideration → web; high-touch → store.

03

Sequence. Invest by constraint removal, not by hype. Example: fix mobile checkout before launching live shopping — mobile cart abandonment runs near 80%.

04

Instrument. Give every channel its own unit economics — CAC, average order value, contribution margin, repeat rate — so channels are compared honestly.

05

Review annually. Channels decay (organic reach) and channels emerge (AI agents). Plan for migration across the continuum, not permanence in any era.

## Slide 37

TOPIC 1 · MINI CASE · PINDUODUO

Born in era 4: Pinduoduo skipped the web store entirely

CONTEXT

Founded 2015/16 in China — two decades after Amazon, in a market where Alibaba and JD already dominated conventional e-commerce. Competing on search-based catalogue shopping was a losing game.

THE MECHANISM

Team purchase: prices drop when buyers form groups — so shoppers recruit friends and family on WeChat to unlock deals. Discovery, social proof and acquisition all happen inside the chat thread, not in a search box.

SCALE

~900M+ users — reaching a top-3 position among Chinese platforms within a few years of launch, powered by social virality rather than advertising spend.

Companion case: Meesho (India, 2015) applied the same social-selling logic to ~1.4M+ small sellers via WhatsApp.

THE LESSON

Each era creates natives that the previous era's giants struggle to copy. Alibaba and JD built era-2 muscles; Pinduoduo built era-4 muscles. When the interface changes, the organisational advantage resets — that is why incumbents buy (Meta/Instagram) or copy (JD's group deals) rather than originate.

## Slide 38

TOPIC 1 · MISCONCEPTION CHECK

"E-commerce killed physical retail" — did it, though?

Evidence that sounds like "yes"

E-commerce took ~20% of world retail in 30 years — an enormous absolute transfer of spend

COVID jumped US online share from 10.6% to 14.5% in a single year (2020)

Category casualties are real: bookstores, electronics and toys moved online first

Evidence that says "no"

~80% of global retail is still transacted in person (2025)

The COVID bump partly reverted: US share went 14.5% (2020) → 14.4% (2022) — a pull-forward, not a permanent step

US growth returned to single digits by 2022–2025

The biggest "physical" retailer became #2 online (Walmart)

THE NUANCED CONCLUSION

The story is integration, not replacement. Channels layer into a portfolio: stores became fulfilment nodes, showrooms and return points while digital captured discovery and convenience. Model it as migration across a continuum — and be suspicious of any "X is dead" headline.

## Slide 41

TOPIC 1 · SUMMARY

Five takeaways, each with a name you can cite

1

The retail evolution continuum. Five eras — store, web, mobile, social, agentic — each adding an interface, none deleting the last. Strategy = managing a portfolio, not picking a winner.

2

Constraints, not dates. Each era removed a shopper constraint — shelf space, location, attention, effort, delegation. Predict the next era by asking which constraint remains.

3

Rogers' Diffusion of Innovation (1962). Adoption follows 2.5 / 13.5 / 34 / 34 / 16 — an S-curve. AI discovery is crossing the chasm (39% usage); AI payment delegation is still in the innovators.

4

Lock-in beats arrival order. First movers win on network effects and data (Amazon, Alibaba, UPI); fast followers win when pioneers pay for education (Google, Walmart+, Flipkart). "First" is not a moat.

5

Architecture is strategy. Headless commerce — one engine, many heads via APIs — is the quiet prerequisite for agentic commerce. AI agents buy through data and APIs, not web pages.
