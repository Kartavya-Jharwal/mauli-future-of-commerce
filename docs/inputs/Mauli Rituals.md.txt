**How AI assistants describe Mauli Rituals today?**

We tested how two AI shopping assistants, Gemini and ChatGPT, answer ten realistic shopper questions. The questions covered sleep body oils, Ayurvedic hair oil, Vata body oil, spa gifts, whether Mauli is worth the money, a comparison with Aesop, whether Sacred Union is scientifically proven, beginner products, where to buy in London, and a beginner routine. We checked each answer on four points: whether Mauli is named, whether any price is correct, whether dosha or sensory language is used, and whether any claim is unsupported or wrong. We also checked prices against the live website and the August 2026 retailer deck.

**What we found**

* **Mauli is named in 6 of 10 Gemini answers and 7 of 10 ChatGPT answers.** Mauli appears most when the question includes Ayurveda, dosha or Vata. It is absent from generic category questions such as "best Ayurvedic hair oil", where the assistants recommend other brands.  
* **ChatGPT's prices were correct in all five checkable cases.** For example, it cited Surrender Vata at £48 and Sacred Union at £88, both matching the site.  
* **Gemini made errors.** It gave the wrong price for Grow Strong Hair Oil (£39–£44, where the site shows £35). It also named a gift set and a body brush that we could not find on the Mauli website, and it described the oils as "100% natural, cold-pressed, therapeutic-grade", which the site does not say.  
* **Scientific claims were rated differently.** ChatGPT found no clinical trial supporting the finished Sacred Union product. Gemini presented ingredient-level evidence as if it applied to the product.

**What this means for the brand**

The dosha and ritual route is where Mauli is already visible to AI assistants, so it is the right place to build. The hair category is not being recommended, and AI assistants are repeating claims that are not on the site. Both need attention, including a clear and current set of product facts that assistants can read.

This is an early read. Each assistant was tested once with default settings, so answers may vary on repeat. The price checks used the live site and the deck rather than every product page.

**Evidence key**

* **Strong:** a real study with a stated method.  
* **Moderate:** real data, but dated, single-case, from one agency, or from a vendor.  
* **Hypothesis:** no published data found. The step includes the test that would settle it.

#### **0\. Context analysis (COSTAC step 1\)**

1. **Pull Mauli’s own numbers.** Get channel-level spend, cost per acquisition, first-order contribution, and the current split between brand-building and sales activation.  
   * *Evidence (Strong, method):* The course’s COSTAC plan starts with context analysis. Binet and Field’s 60/40 guideline can only be checked against a real split ([LeadGen Economy](https://www.leadgen-economy.com/blog/brand-performance-balance-binet-field-60-40/)).

2. **Map the five consumer factors to data sources.** Personal, cultural, social, psychological and situational each get a named data source: quiz answers, purchase history, referral source, ritual log, and calendar events.  
   * *Evidence (Moderate):* The course lists the five factors. The UK Mother’s Day forecast is a real situational signal (£18bn for 2026, up about 15%), but it comes from a 1,000-person agency survey ([Retail Gazette](https://www.retailgazette.co.uk/?p=200896)).

#### **1\. Differentiate (D): own the dosha ritual**

1. Write ritual specs for the six core products, with a named human author and practitioner sign-off.  
   * *Evidence (Strong, legal):* Claims about wellbeing have to be substantiated. The CMA’s Emma Sleep case shows that unsubstantiated urgency and claims are enforced ([Gowling WLG](https://gowlingwlg.com/fr/insights-resources/articles/2026/misleading-sales-practices-dont-sleep-on-the-cma)).  
2. Run a claims audit across site, feeds and press pages. Remove “scientifically proven” unless the evidence file supports it.  
   * *Evidence (Strong, legal):* same source.  
3. Build the mother-care story as a documented heritage line, with real family and founder material.  
   * *Evidence (Moderate):* Ritson’s analysis of 18,230 System1-tested ads found that brand-building ads often drive short-term sales too, roughly nine times in ten by System1’s estimate ([Marketing Week](https://www.marketingweek.com/ritson-brand-building-boost-short-term)). This is a vendor’s panel, not Mauli’s audience.

#### **2\. Remind (R): keep Mauli top of mind**

1. Launch the newsletter as the spine: ritual reminders, refill prompts, and seasonal rituals.  
   * *Evidence (Strong, course):* The course says consumers have short memories in a crowded market and need repeated reminders.  
   * *Evidence (Moderate):* The deck reports a 77% newsletter open rate. That is client-stated and unaudited.  
2. Pilot a refill subscription on the Supreme Skin Serum and Grow Strong Hair Oil, with a 10% member price against a full-price control.  
   * *Evidence (Hypothesis):* No real subscription uptake data for this category. The test sets the answer.

#### **3\. Inform (I): meet people who are searching**

1. Keep Google Ads only on intent terms that describe the problem, such as dosha-related or ritual-related searches. Point each to the quiz, not to a product page.  
   * *Evidence (Strong, course):* The course’s information-search stage says firms should provide information before customers come looking, and that inform targets people who have never bought the brand.  
   * *Evidence (Hypothesis on profit):* I haven’t found UK cost-per-click data for these terms. Judge the spend on Mauli’s own cost per acquisition, not on assumption.  
2. Cut Meta reach spend by a fixed share, starting at 15%, and redirect it to the ritual and membership lines. Hold the cut only if the metrics move.  
   * *Evidence (Strong, course):* DRIPE and the 3Ps say to match spend to the job each channel does. The 60/40 split says activation past 70% gives short-term gains and long-term decline ([LeadGen Economy](https://www.leadgen-economy.com/blog/brand-performance-balance-binet-field-60-40/)).

#### **4\. Persuade (P): sell the benefit, not the discount**

1. Replace the two −20% events with a free ritual trial and a dosha bundle at full price, starting with the next sale window.  
   * *Evidence (Strong, course):* The course says discounts erode profit, make the product look inferior, and teach people to wait for promotions.  
   * *Evidence (Strong, course):* “You don’t buy the brand, you buy the benefits.”  
2. Track full-price revenue share after the first window and compare contribution with and without the bundle.  
   * *Evidence (Hypothesis):* No published dataset on Mauli’s price elasticity. The test sets the answer.

---

#### **5\. Engage (E): turn customers into a practice**

1. Launch a free practice membership: seasonal rituals, a monthly live session with a practitioner, and a tracked ritual log.  
   * *Evidence (Strong, course):* Engagement tactics in the course are memberships, customer care, referral programmes and virtual communities.  
2. Launch a referral programme with a modest, flat reward.  
   * *Evidence (Moderate):* Schmitt, Skiera and Van den Bulte’s study of 5,181 referred customers at a German bank found 18% lower churn hazard and about 25% higher observed value. It is one bank, one country, one year, and the reward was a flat €25 ([paper](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2012/04/Schmitt-Skiera-vandenBulte-2011-Referral-Programs-Customer-Value.pdf)).  
3. Measure the referral programme against a control group that never sees the offer.  
   * *Evidence (Strong, method):* the study used matched comparison groups for the same reason.

1. Use the newsletter, practitioner network and the existing retail partners to carry the ritual. Add no new wholesale until first-order contribution is positive, as the brief already says.

But Mauli's value is a ritual practised over time, and nothing in the brief shows those moments being captured or followed up. In the course's Consumer Decision Journey, this is a missing **loyalty loop**: a strong post-purchase experience lets customers skip re-evaluation and return straight to purchase (McKinsey, 2009\) \[COURSE\].

This one gap explains the other constraints:

* **First-order break-even** hurts because the second and third orders are not engineered.  
* **The halo without profit** comes from having no route from the hotel room to an owned relationship.  
* **Founder dependency** exists because knowledge lives in people, not in reusable journeys.

**Solve first:** the missing link between the dosha knowledge Mauli has and the customer record it holds.

**This diagnosis is wrong if** Mauli already runs dosha-segmented journeys, or its repeat rate is already high for the category (Sections Q and S).

## **K. MVP Recommendation**

**The MVP is a 90-day Ritual Profile pilot.** Store quiz answers (dosha \+ main concern) on the customer record. Send three dosha-specific post-purchase journeys in Anita's voice to new buyers and quiz-takers, and compare them against a holdout group. This tests the founder's personalisation question before any AI platform is built: if dosha-matched rituals don't change repeat behaviour, a concierge wouldn't either.

| Step | What Mauli does |
| ----- | ----- |
| **1\. Test** | Pass quiz answers into the email/CRM platform as profile fields. Write three journeys (Vata / Pitta / Kapha), each 4–5 emails over \~6 weeks: how to start, the sensory "why", a technique video, a check-in, and the natural next ritual step. Applying Fogg's model \[COURSE\]: the last email is a replenishment prompt timed to when the product runs out. No discounts. Invite buyers with no dosha on file to take the quiz. |
| **2\. Measure** | Hold out \~20% as a control, because attribution ≠ causation \[COURSE\]. Track second-order rate, time to second order, quiz completion, ritual adoption, full-price share and unsubscribes. |
| **3\. Learn** | Which doshas convert, which steps are skipped, and which questions customers ask. The question log becomes the training set and guardrails for a later AI advisor. |
| **4\. Scale or stop** | **Scale** if the profiled group's 90-day second-order rate beats control at full price and journeys need only a few hours a month. **Stop** if there is no measurable uplift with adequate samples, or quiz uptake is negligible. **Phase 2 condition:** the uplift holds in a second cohort. |

**Pilot specification:**

* **Customer:** new D2C buyers and quiz-takers, the 35+ "experimental traditionalists" \[CLIENT\].  
* **Problem:** "I bought something beautiful; how do I make it a ritual that works for me?"  
* **Technology:** the existing e-commerce platform plus an email/CRM tool with profile fields and triggered flows. Buy, don't build. If the stack can't hold profile fields, that is the first fix.  
* **People:** an owner in marketing (Erin's function \[CLIENT\]), one recorded content sprint with Anita, \~1 day a week of support, and an optional freelance CRM specialist for setup.  
* **Data created:** dosha, concern, ritual stage, origin channel and repeat behaviour, all owned.  
* **New metric:** **ritual adoption rate**, the % of buyers who buy a second step of their dosha ritual within 90 days. It passes the course's three tests \[COURSE\]: measurable with data Mauli already has, tied to a non-negotiable (ritual over SKU), and still meaningful on future platforms. Follower counts and open rates are deliberately not KPIs, per the Draw's own ban \[COURSE\].

## **P. Contrarian Insight**

**For Mauli, personalisation is worth most after the purchase, not before it. The best year-one "AI investment" therefore has no chatbot in it.** \[INTERPRETATION\]

**Conventional advice:** put an AI dosha concierge on the homepage, buy more paid social, and go to TikTok for reach.

**Why it is probably wrong for Mauli:**

1. **The ritual happens after purchase.** A pre-purchase recommender improves one decision. Post-purchase journeys shape the dozens of uses that decide whether a customer returns. This is the CDJ loyalty loop \[COURSE\].  
2. **Mauli doesn't need more reach; it leaks the reach it has.** More exposure on a system that doesn't convert exposure buys more leakage, at Meta prices rising 12% a year \[EXTERNAL\].  
3. **The AI-readiness work and the retention work are the same work.** Structured dosha profiles and ritual content are what a concierge, a therapist portal and AI shopping agents all need \[EXTERNAL\]\[COURSE: AEO Truth \+ Structure\].  
4. **Digital should carry context, not imitate the senses.** Scent and touch are Mauli's strongest memory codes and the least digitisable \[COURSE: Senses deck\]. Physical touchpoints carry them; journeys carry the context and memory between them. That beats AR or VR trying to simulate them.

**Corollary:** hospitality's value is not as a sales channel. It is an **acquisition environment where luxury partners pay for the trial**. Mauli's job is to provide the route home.

## **Q. Why This Recommendation Could Be Wrong**

**The recommendation rests on internal facts we haven't seen.** If Mauli's repeat engine is already strong or its list is small, the bottleneck shifts to acquisition.

### **Three weaknesses**

1. **It doesn't look like "future commerce".** CRM can read as unglamorous next to AI and AR. The answer is to frame it as the AI foundation, which it is.  
2. **List size may cap the upside.** A small base means small absolute gains.  
3. **Dosha may not drive behaviour.** Customers may enjoy the quiz as content without wanting dosha-specific rituals, in which case simpler product journeys would do as well.

### **Three assumptions**

1. Quiz answers can be captured and linked to customer records with current tools.  
2. Mauli does not already run dosha-segmented lifecycle journeys.  
3. The team can find roughly one recorded content sprint from Anita and about a day a week of ongoing ownership.

**Missing information:** list size, repeat baseline, quiz volume and completion, current flows, platforms, partner-level sales, CAC and contribution margin, and team capacity.

**Alternative explanation:** the 54% existing share may mean retention is *already* Mauli's strength. On the CDJ, its customers might be active loyalists rather than passive ones \[COURSE\]. Then the constraint is a narrow top of funnel, and the hospitality bridge or therapists should lead.

**What would overturn it:** a repeat rate already high for luxury skincare with dosha journeys in place; no uplift against control after 90 days; or strong, high-value therapist demand from the waitlist.

**Three pieces of information that would most raise confidence:** (1) the 90-day and 12-month repeat rates; (2) the platforms in use, list size, and whether quiz answers are stored per customer; (3) who would own this, and their monthly hours.

## **R. Final One-Investment Decision**

**If Mauli could fund only ONE strategic initiative over the next 12 months:**

**We would invest in:** a dosha-based **Ritual Profile** with post-purchase ritual journeys, turning every purchase, quiz and partner touch into a personalised, repeat ritual relationship.

**Executed primarily through:** **email/CRM on the D2C platform**, with QR on the red-thread card and spa amenities as entry points.

**Because:**

1. The first D2C order barely breaks even and 72% of spend is on Meta \[CLIENT\], where the price per ad rose 12% year on year through 2026 \[EXTERNAL\]. Profit depends on second and later orders. The CDJ loyalty loop is where they are won \[COURSE\].

Automated flows earn \~18x more revenue per recipient than campaigns \[EXTERNAL, vendor\]. It moves Mauli up the personalisation maturity model without new behaviour from customers \[COURSE\].  
3. Structured profiles and ritual content are the foundation that AI concierges and agentic commerce require \[EXTERNAL: McKinsey\]\[COURSE: AEO\]. Most AI built without that foundation shows no P\&L impact \[EXTERNAL: MIT NANDA\], so this investment compounds into everything after it.

**We would deliberately NOT prioritise yet:** **A customer-facing AI ritual concierge**

**because:** it needs the profile data, approved content and proof of dosha-led behaviour that the Ritual Profile creates. Built first, it would spend Mauli's scarcest resource, attention, on a high-risk build that could misrepresent Ayurveda and flatten the personal touch: the founder's own core fear.

**We would reconsider this recommendation if:**

1. Mauli's data shows repeat buying is already strong and dosha journeys already exist. Then acquisition is the bottleneck, and hospitality or therapists should lead.

# **Mauli Rituals — RQ3 Summary: Where to Execute With Limited Resources**

## **1\. The question and the answer in brief**

Anita Kaushal asked the class: "If we have limited resources, where is the best place for us to execute, and how?" This summary condenses our full research, which draws on the Client Challenge Brief, independent 2024–2026 evidence, peer-reviewed marketing research and the Future of Commerce course slides.

**Our answer is to execute first in Mauli's own email and CRM, on the D2C site, by building a dosha-based Ritual Profile.** The profile stores each customer's dosha and main concern. It then drives post-purchase ritual journeys written in Anita's voice.

The logic is simple. Mauli does not lack reach or prestige. It lacks a system that turns the trust it already creates into repeat, profitable relationships. The course's own client deck puts it best: Mauli has "an attention-cost problem wearing a tech costume."

We would deliberately not prioritise a customer-facing AI concierge, AR, VR, an app, retail expansion or extra Meta spend yet. Each of these either needs data Mauli does not have, or adds work its small team cannot sustain.

## **2\. Where Mauli stands today**

Mauli is a ten-year-old British Ayurvedic luxury brand with an £87 average order, 74 awards, and partners such as Bulgari (nine years), Four Seasons, Lamborghini and Emirates. D2C is 61% of the business; spa, retail and amenities make up the rest. All figures here are founder-stated and unaudited.

Each channel plays a different role. **D2C is the profit engine** but barely breaks even on the first order once discounts and Meta costs are paid. **Spa and hospitality partners build the brand**, yet Anita calls them "nice to have, but not that profitable" with "no compounding effect." **Retail is costly**: Space NK asks for a 65% margin plus marketing contributions. **Instagram grew from 36k to 105k** in a year, but Anita doubts it is a real community.

Marketing spend is 72% Meta and 28% Google. Two −20% sales a year have trained customers to wait for discounts, and the founder wants to wean them off slowly.

Mauli's strongest assets are already in its hands. The dosha questionnaire gives real value with no sales push. The newsletter has a 77% open rate. 54% of business comes from existing customers. Every boxed product carries a blessed red thread. And Anita's knowledge of Ayurveda is deep. These assets are mostly disconnected from each other, which is the key to everything that follows.

What we do not know matters too. The brief does not disclose revenue, margins, team size, technology stack, list size, or how many people complete the quiz.

## **3\. The constraints and the hidden bottleneck**

We ranked three constraints by how much each limits growth.

**First, first-order economics.** Each new D2C customer arrives at roughly zero margin because acquisition is rented from Meta, and Meta's average price per ad rose 12% year on year in both Q1 and Q2 2026\. Discounting makes this worse: research shows repeated promotions make customers more price-sensitive over time (Mela, Gupta and Lehmann, 1997). Technology can raise the value of each customer, but it cannot make Meta cheaper.

**Second, no connected customer intelligence.** Nothing suggests quiz answers are linked to purchases or emails, and Mauli's tech is "deliberately untouched." Hotel guests experience the full ritual, then leave with no route back. Without this foundation, any future AI tool sits on empty data, which is why about 95% of corporate generative-AI pilots show no measurable profit impact (MIT NANDA, 2025).

**Third, organisational attention.** Mauli runs five channels with a small team, outsources social media, and is stepping Anita back from being the face of the brand. Anita's own worst case is "not always being as coherent as we would like… and not feeling authentic." Every new tool competes for the same few people.

**The hidden bottleneck connects all three: the ritual relationship ends at the first touch.** Mauli creates high-trust moments, such as a spa treatment, a quiz result or an unboxing, but has no system to carry them forward. Since its value is a ritual practised over time, this gap explains the thin first-order profit, the halo without profit and the dependence on Anita.

## **4\. What the external evidence says**

**Paid reach keeps getting more expensive.** Meta's own filings show price per ad rising 12% a year, and agency data across 230+ brands shows Meta return on ad spend falling from 2.57x to 1.56x between 2024 and 2026\.

**Owned automation is the most efficient lever.** Klaviyo's benchmarks (a vendor source) show automated flows earn about 41% of email revenue from just 5.3% of sends, roughly 18 times more per recipient than one-off campaigns. McKinsey finds personalisation typically lifts revenue 10–15%, though results depend on data quality. Peer-reviewed work also finds that a 1% improvement in retention raises customer value by 3–7% (Gupta, Lehmann and Stuart, 2004).

**AI pays off only on clean, connected data.** McKinsey projects AI shopping agents could orchestrate \$3–5 trillion of global commerce by 2030 and says brands with structured data will be favoured. Adobe recorded AI-referred retail traffic up 693% in holiday 2025, converting 31% better. But generative-AI search was still only 3.3% of search time in 2025, so this is something to prepare for cheaply, not bet on.

**Expensive technology bets often fail.** Lululemon paid \$500m for the Mirror and wrote most of it off. Sephora closed its community forums, and Shiseido shut its metaverse project. AR works well when customers need to see a result on themselves, as with Ulta's make-up try-on, but Mauli's rituals are felt and smelled rather than seen.

**Competitors confirm the pattern.** Dermalogica grew through trained professional therapists, Susanne Kaufmann through a handful of deep spa partnerships, and Ranavat (listed by BeautyMatter as self-funded) through education-led content before retail. None of them grew through a technology layer.

## **5\. What the course frameworks add**

**Channels layer; they don't replace (Week 1).** The retail evolution continuum shows every interface since 1994 is still in use. Strategy is about the mix, which supports connecting Mauli's existing channels rather than adding an app or VR. The Mauli Draw debrief makes the same point: "eight different problems, one connected system."

**Move first only where lock-in exists (Week 1).** First movers win when data or network effects lock customers in. Mauli should be first with its own dosha data, and a fast follower on agentic checkout and AR, where others pay to educate the market. Rogers' diffusion theory adds that low-trialability technologies, like a new app, stall.

**The loyalty loop is where value is won (Week 2).** McKinsey's Consumer Decision Journey shows a strong post-purchase experience lets customers skip re-evaluation and buy again. It also separates active loyalists from passive ones who can be poached. Some of Mauli's 54% repeat base may be passive, and post-purchase rituals could make them active.

**Behaviour needs ability and a timed prompt (Week 2).** Fogg's B=MAP model says the easiest action at the right moment wins. A reorder reminder sent when a product runs out could replace the −20% sale as the trigger to buy.

**Climb the personalisation staircase in order (Week 2).** The maturity model runs from segments to 1:1 rules to prediction to agents, and each stage funds the next stage's data. Mauli sits at stage one; the Ritual Profile moves it to stage two. The quiz solves the "cold start" problem, and Stitch Fix's quiz-to-algorithm-to-human model mirrors quiz-to-journey-to-Anita or a therapist. Next-best-action thinking adds that "educate" often beats "discount", and the privacy rules say data collection must give the customer clear, immediate value.

**Make the ritual readable by AI (AEO/GEO deck).** AI engines cite what they can extract and trust. The cheapest first layers are "Truth" and "Structure": accurate, structured product and ritual data. This directly answers Anita's fear that AI will flatten her ritual into a SKU.

**Digital should carry context, not imitate the senses (Senses deck).** Smell is the sense most tied to memory yet the hardest to digitise, and touch drives the feeling of ownership. Mauli's physical touchpoints already carry scent and touch, so digital's job is to carry the context and memory between them.

## **6\. Options tested and how they scored**

We tested nine competing hypotheses against the evidence. Three survived, and together they form one strategy: **retention first** (focus on existing customers), **infrastructure before customer-facing AI** (build the data foundation first), and **integrate rather than add** (connect existing channels into one customer record). D2C is the venue, and the hospitality-to-D2C bridge is the cheapest new entry point.

The therapist network is strong but staged. Demand is real and unprompted, and a vetted network would be hard to copy, as Dermalogica shows. But vetting and supporting therapists is permanent work, so it gets a cheap demand test now and investment later.

AI personalisation first is the right idea in the wrong order: there is no profile data to personalise on yet. Community first should wait, because owned forums are declining and Mauli has no voice yet that works without Anita. AR first is rejected for now, since Mauli's problem is not about seeing a result.

We then scored 14 initiatives on 12 factors, from impact and cost to brand fit and lasting value, on a 1–5 scale. **The Ritual Profile with CRM journeys scored 54 out of 60**, eight points clear. It reuses assets Mauli already owns, is easy to measure, and leaves Mauli owning the data and content.

## **7\. The recommendation and the 90-day test**

**If Mauli could fund only one initiative in the next 12 months, it should be the Ritual Profile, executed through email and CRM on the D2C site.** QR codes on the red-thread card and spa amenities act as extra entry points.

Three reasons support it. First, profit depends on second and later orders, because the first order barely breaks even and Meta costs keep rising. Second, Mauli already owns every input, so the work is connecting assets, not buying new ones. Third, structured profiles and ritual content are the foundation that any future AI concierge, therapist portal or AI-agent visibility will need, so the investment compounds.

**Our contrarian insight is that personalisation is worth most after purchase, not before it.** Most marketers would put an AI concierge on the homepage and buy more reach. But Mauli's ritual happens at home, over weeks. Hospitality is best seen as an acquisition environment where luxury partners pay for the trial; Mauli's job is to provide the route home.

**The smallest useful test is a 90-day pilot.** Mauli stores quiz answers (dosha and main concern) on each customer record. It writes three short email journeys, one each for Vata, Pitta and Kapha, in Anita's voice: how to start, why it works, a technique video, a check-in, and the natural next step. The final email is a reorder prompt timed to when the product runs out, with no discount.

About 20% of eligible customers are held back as a control group, because attribution is not the same as causation. The team tracks second-order rate, time to second order, quiz completion and unsubscribes.

We propose one new metric: **ritual adoption rate**, the share of buyers who buy a second step of their dosha ritual within 90 days. It passes the course's three tests: Mauli can measure it with data it already has, it is tied to a non-negotiable (ritual over SKU), and it will still matter on future platforms.

**Mauli should scale** if the profiled group out-buys the control at full price and the journeys need only a few hours a month. **It should stop** if there is no measurable difference. The pilot needs a marketing owner, one recorded content session with Anita, and existing email software; nothing custom is built.

## **8\. Do now, do next, explore later**

**Do now (about 0–6 months), three moves that form one system:**

* **The Ritual Profile and dosha journeys**, with the ritual library also published as structured product data so AI engines can read it.  
* **A partner-to-profile QR pilot.** One QR code on the red-thread card and at one spa partner leads to "continue your ritual at home" and the quiz. Success is measured in scans per thousand units, and in how many scans become profiles and first orders.  
* **Replacing one −20% sale with ritual value**, such as a free Kansa or roller step for one customer segment. This tests whether customers respond to added value without a price cut.

A therapist waitlist and application form also goes live now, at almost no cost, to measure real demand.

**Do next (about 6–18 months)** builds on what Phase 1 creates: a lean therapist programme with vetted trade tiers, an AI ritual advisor trained only on approved content with a human handoff, replenishment reminders and later opt-in subscriptions, small 21-day ritual cohorts run over email and Instagram, and a stronger presence in AI answers through reviews and press. The pace depends on team capacity Mauli has not yet shared.

**Explore later:** AR, an app, VR, digital scent, retail expansion, TikTok Shop and the US market. Each becomes worth revisiting only if something changes, such as evidence that customers struggle with technique, a partner willing to co-fund, or better retail terms.

**The sequence is foundation, then extend, then assist.** Phase 1 creates profiles, a structured ritual library and a repeat-rate baseline. Phase 2 adds therapists and a log of customer questions. Phase 3 uses all of that to power an AI advisor and one shared customer record across D2C, spas and therapists. A gate sits between each phase: if the evidence fails, Mauli stops and moves the hours elsewhere.

## **9\. Risks, what could prove us wrong, and questions for Mauli**

**Most risks on this path are low to medium.** The most likely are that journeys get written and then neglected, and that quiz uptake is low. Mauli can reduce these by giving the work one owner, keeping flows evergreen, keeping the quiz short and inviting customers at high-intent moments.

Privacy and brand risks need care. Mauli should ask explicit consent, collect only dosha and concern (never inferred health conditions), and apply the course's "creepiness test." Using Anita's recorded voice, avoiding discount triggers and framing doshas as a starting point rather than a diagnosis protect the luxury feel and cultural integrity. Wellbeing-advice risks mainly affect the later AI advisor, which is why the content is written by people first.

**The recommendation could be wrong in three ways.** CRM may look unglamorous next to AI and AR, so it must be framed as the AI foundation it really is. A small customer list would limit the upside. And customers may enjoy the quiz without wanting dosha-specific rituals.

There is also an alternative explanation. The 54% repeat share may mean retention is already Mauli's strength. If so, the real constraint is a narrow top of funnel, and the hospitality bridge or therapists should lead instead.

**Three answers from Mauli would most improve our confidence:**

* What are the 90-day and 12-month repeat-purchase rates?  
* Which e-commerce and email platforms does Mauli use, how big is the list, and are quiz answers stored per customer?  
* Who would own this work, and how many hours a month can they give it?

We would also ask about quiz completion, current automated emails, margins and acquisition costs, which partners drive any D2C sales, how many therapists have asked to buy, and what share of revenue the two sales bring in.

# **Subscription design and treatment economics**

## **Choose replenishment rather than a discovery box**

A replenishment subscription delivers selected products regularly; a discovery box sends a changing assortment; a paid membership charges for access to benefits. Anita’s reported demand most directly supports replenishment. It does not establish willingness to pay an additional membership fee. Therapists should be able to choose products, quantities and intervals, with clear options to change, skip, pause or cancel.

McKinsey’s 2018 study surveyed 5,093 US respondents, including 607 subscription customers. Among replenishment subscribers, convenience was the most important reason for continuing for 24%, value for money for 23% and personalisation for 22%. The research also identified accumulated products and inability to adjust quantities as cancellation problems. This is older US consumer evidence, not a forecast for UK therapists; it supports flexibility as a design principle.

Source: McKinsey & Company, Thinking inside the subscription box. [https\://www\.mckinsey.com/industries/technology-media-and-telecommunications/our-insights/thinking-inside-the-subscription-box-new-research-on-ecommerce-consumers](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/technology-media-and-telecommunications/our-insights/thinking-inside-the-subscription-box-new-research-on-ecommerce-consumers)

## **Select products using actual treatment consumption**

Recommendation: assess massage oils and frequently used facial products first, using existing therapist orders and enquiries. Offer durable Kansa tools (the facial massage tools) as initial or occasional purchases rather than routine replenishment. Assess professional pack sizes, storage, shelf life and wastage before recommending larger quantities.

## **Explain cost per treatment**

Eve Taylor publishes approximate product costs of £1.46 for a mini facial and £2.00 for an essential facial, with approximately 140 treatments from its featured starter pack. It warns that consumption varies though. These are supplier estimates of product cost, not the total cost of providing a treatment or a pricing target for Mauli.

For each product: cost per treatment \= professional pack price ÷ usable pack volume × volume used per treatment. Add the amounts for every product used. Mauli-approved dosage and wastage allowances are needed before publishing reliable figures.

## **Price for contribution rather than copying discounts**

Net sales excluding VAT minus product cost, fulfilment, delivery subsidy, payment/platform charges and attributable support costs gives contribution before remaining fixed overheads. Professional pricing and any extra subscription discount must be assessed together. Recurring revenue does not automatically mean profitable revenue.

Recommendation: base purchasing tiers on volume and cost to serve, with common quality standards across all tiers. Exact prices, thresholds and discounts remain undecided until Mauli supplies cost and margin data. Higher spending should not imply greater professional competence.

 

# **Vetting and practical technology**

## **Verify qualifications and insurance**

Neal’s Yard Remedies states that its practitioner discount card lasts two years and requires a current professional insurance certificate for renewal. Together with Eve Taylor’s manual checks, this supports verification at entry and periodic renewal, rather than permanent approval after one application.

Source: Neal’s Yard Remedies, Help centre. [https\://www\.nealsyardremedies.com/pages/faq](https://www.nealsyardremedies.com/pages/faq)

FHT says its therapist register includes eligible members holding professional qualifications and insurance; students, associates and friends are excluded (Federation of Holistic Therapists, 2023). BABTAC (British Association of Beauty Therapy & Cosmetology**)** reports over 8,000 members but offers membership with or without insurance, so membership alone should not establish current cover.

Source: FHT, Making the most out of your FHT membership. [https\://blog.fht.org.uk/2023/04/05/making-the-most-out-of-your-fht-membership/](https://blog.fht.org.uk/2023/04/05/making-the-most-out-of-your-fht-membership/)

Source: BABTAC, About BABTAC. [https\://www\.babtac.com/about](https://www.babtac.com/about)

Recommended process: application; qualification and relevant insurance checks; product and ritual induction; account approval; expiry reminders. AI could extract document details or flag missing information, while a person verifies suitability. Mauli product training should be distinguished from an independent professional qualification.

## **Check trade pricing and recurring payments together**

Shopify supports B2B accounts, catalogues, quantity rules and volume pricing, but its official documentation currently lists subscriptions as incompatible with native B2B. It would be incorrect to promise that an ordinary subscription app automatically resolves this limitation.

Source: Shopify, Requirements and considerations for using B2B. [https\://help.shopify.com/en/manual/b2b/getting-started/considerations](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/b2b/getting-started/considerations)

Wholesale Suite documents an integration between WooCommerce Subscriptions and Wholesale Prices Premium that applies professional pricing to subscription products and recurring charges. This is a documented vendor option, not independent verification or a reason to migrate Mauli’s website without assessing costs.

Source: Wholesale Suite, WooCommerce Subscriptions integration. [https\://wholesalesuiteplugin.com/integrations/woocommerce-subscriptions-integration/](https://wholesalesuiteplugin.com/integrations/woocommerce-subscriptions-integration/)

Before choosing tools, confirm Mauli’s current platform, plan and integrations. Test initial and renewal prices, quantity changes, cancellations, failed payments, stock shortages and expired approvals. Order/payment automation is the foundation; AI forecasting can follow when sufficient consumption data exists.

 

# **Pilot proposal and measures of success**

Recommendation: recruit a manageable initial cohort from therapists who have already expressed interest. The pilot size should follow the actual enquiry list and available support capacity. Run long enough to observe scheduled renewals for each delivery interval; a first order alone cannot establish subscription retention.

## **Collect before launch**

Ask interested therapists which products and sizes they need, treatment volume, quantities consumed, current ordering intervals, preferred delivery schedules, acceptable prices and delivery charges, and reasons for wanting a subscription. Verify qualifications and insurance. Use Mauli’s product costs, lead times and professional-size availability to price the offer.

## **Measure commercial and operational results**

**Subscription conversion.** Paying subscribers divided by approved therapists offered the programme.

**Scheduled renewal success.** Paid and fulfilled renewals divided by renewals due; report voluntary skips, cancellations and failed payments separately.

**Contribution per therapist.** Net revenue less attributable variable costs over the pilot, with onboarding costs recorded separately.

**Administration time.** Staff minutes per application and per order compared with the existing process.

**Supply reliability.** Orders delivered complete and on time divided by orders due.

**Incremental contribution.** Contribution compared with the same therapists’ previous ordinary purchases, accounting for seasonality and purchasing frequency.

Moving existing orders into subscriptions may improve convenience without increasing sales. The pilot should establish whether the benefit comes from larger purchases, retained customers, reduced administration or some combination. Growth targets should be set after a baseline is available, rather than invented from competitor offers.

## **Protect the brand and prioritise resources**

Keep formulas and approved ritual guidance consistent. Start with a narrow professional catalogue and accessible human support. Training and optional live demonstrations can support the subscription, while the core value remains reliable product supply. Exclude a public therapist-booking marketplace, compulsory paid membership and fully automated therapist approval from the first phase.

## **Connection to the wider assignment**

The brief requires proposals across at least two specified technology areas. If the group chooses this B2B path, AI-personalised replenishment suggestions and live professional demonstrations with purchasing links are possible extensions. These are proposed applications, not established Mauli capabilities. Their value and resource requirements should be tested rather than assumed.

## **Decision for the group**

Support a paid pilot of vetted, flexible therapist product replenishment. Do not claim that subscriptions guarantee profit or that reported interest establishes market size. The missing evidence is Mauli-specific conversion, usage, trade margins and successful paid renewals.
