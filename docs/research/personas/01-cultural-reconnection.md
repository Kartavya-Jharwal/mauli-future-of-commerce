# Umbrella 1 — Anika, heritage-connected ritual seeker

**Fictional research composite, 10 October 2026.** Every biographical detail and spending limit below is an invented scenario. No participant has been interviewed for this profile. It is not a typical South Asian customer or an established Mauli segment. [Architecture and sources](../stp-and-icps.md).

## Concrete person and qualifying job

Anika is 37, British-born with Indian family roots, living in London with a partner and a young child. She works four days a week in a professional role. Her household has some discretionary spending, with housing and childcare competing for it. In this scenario she can consider **£60 for one self-care purchase**, only if use and value are clear; this is a test constraint, not a product price or income estimate. She has five minutes on ordinary evenings and more time occasionally. Gender, parenthood and occupation are illustrative, not qualifications.

Scenario voice, not a customer quote: “I remember the care in those routines. I want to make one my own without being told I am doing my culture wrong.”

The qualifying signal is a **voluntarily expressed connection between a present practice and heritage**. Indian ancestry alone does not qualify her. She may be culturally confident: the motivation could be continuity, curiosity or sharing, with no confusion or sense of loss. Ask which description she prefers.

## Self-described ethnographic context to investigate

In the scenario, memories of family hair-oiling feel familiar, while formal dosha terminology does not. Family members may differ about ingredients, paying premium prices or commercialising an everyday practice. She might explain a practice to her partner or child in English and use other words with relatives. These are research prompts, not observations about Indian households.

Ask which person, place, practice, language or region matters; accept none. Ask what she retained, adapted, declined or only recently discovered. Does the same practice feel intimate at home but performative on social media? Do not use language proficiency, religious observance, festivals or visits to India as authenticity tests. Recruit different genders, life stages, regions and mixed-heritage contexts to challenge this single example.

## Psychographics, needs and tensions

| Layer | Hypothesis to explore |
|---|---|
| Functional job | Find an enjoyable, understandable practice and suitable product within time and budget |
| Emotional job | Experience continuity and agency without guilt or a duty to become more culturally authentic |
| Social job | Share meaning if she chooses, without being obliged to explain a whole culture |
| Dream | Carry a personally valued practice into contemporary life while permitting adaptation |
| Need | Clear provenance, practical instructions, transparent ingredients/price and permission to start small |
| Want | Beautiful tactile presentation, founder voice, optional deeper context and thoughtful gifting |
| Anxiety | Exoticised marketing, inflated claims, expensive familiar basics or pressure to purchase belonging |
| Core trade-off | Meaning earns attention but must coexist with sensory fit, usefulness and household priorities |

Emotional triggers to test include a remembered practice, a personally observed celebration, becoming a parent or hearing a relatable founder account. Rational triggers include finishing a product, wanting clearer application guidance, acceptable delivered cost and a trusted recommendation. Do not infer bereavement, insecurity or cultural inadequacy for targeting.

Possible decision tendencies: a familiar ingredient may reduce perceived uncertainty; an origin story may create a quality halo; a relative's recommendation may outweigh advertising. These are testable tendencies, not established biases. Supply product facts and alternatives so she can scrutinise that halo; never intensify guilt or imply that spending proves authenticity.

## Decision journey and preferences

Investigate alternatives: continuing an existing family practice, a simple lower-cost product, another premium brand or doing nothing. Mauli's premium must earn its place. Heritage is not a substitute for comparison.

Anika might arrive through an existing founder story, a personal recommendation or relevant creator content. Ask where the actual journey began; do not assume one diaspora platform. She explores origin, checks a product and instructions, compares total cost and may ask someone she trusts. Preferred vocabulary, fragrance, format, cultural depth and willingness to hear spiritual language remain questions.

The proposed experience offers one practice with optional origin context, readable steps, realistic time and product price. Include a product-free practice and direct browsing. Show who explains the tradition and what is interpretation. The thread/chord visual may connect origin, preference and step if it aids comprehension; it cannot serve as mystical proof of fit.

## Gifting and cultural exploration are occasions here

A birthday, family visit or personally observed festival can be a meaningful-gift occasion. Ask about recipient preferences and practical use rather than relying on thematic packaging. Buyer and recipient have distinct activation events. A curious person without South Asian heritage may value this context too; treat that as an adjacent motivation, not an exact heritage-reconnection ICP. Other diaspora participants may relate to adaptation and intergenerational continuity while needing different cultural references. Mauli does not become an expression of every participant's tradition.

## Content, trust and offer boundaries

Test founder-approved story plus practical demonstration against practical guidance alone. Invite people to make a practice their own. Avoid deficit narratives about lost identity, authenticity rankings, assumed festival observance, unreviewed symbolic interpretation, diagnostic claims or compulsory multiproduct routines. Offer clear origin context without implying that all Indian traditions are interchangeable.

Trust barriers include unclear provenance, generic exotic imagery, a price unexplained by product value, vague instructions and feeling profiled. A visually beautiful story cannot compensate for these. Cultural familiarity might increase scrutiny rather than trust; explicitly ask which claims feel overgeneralised or commercialised.

## Activation and evidence

Activation: selects a step after explanation and can say why it fits her life. Record selection/save separately from voluntary comprehension. Neither proves belonging. Retention includes voluntary use and subsequent replenishment/occasion, recorded separately. Gift-buyer repeat and recipient first use/purchase require distinct cohorts.

Useful declared signals: origin-content interest, practice familiarity, desired explanation depth, chosen step and reason for rejection. Scroll depth is not ethnicity; purchases are not a culture score. Unknown: segment prevalence, willingness to pay, profitable acquisition, incrementality and whether existing customers welcome this emphasis.

## Interviews that can disprove this persona

- Reconstruct the last actual self-care purchase before mentioning heritage. What mattered and what was rejected?
- Ask what feels connected to their life and what feels imposed; accept no connection.
- Ask what they would use if Mauli did not exist, including familiar lower-cost substitutes.
- Invite their own vocabulary and rejected terms. Ask about a family practice they chose not to continue.
- Remove the origin story and ask whether the decision changes; separate representation from product choice.
- Follow up on what they actually did a week later, not only whether they liked the concept.

Disconfirm if participants enjoy representation but prefer existing products, reject reconnection framing or find the story unrelated to choice/use. Keep accurate optional origin information without making a culture-led acquisition claim. Cultural resonance does not itself establish commercial value.
